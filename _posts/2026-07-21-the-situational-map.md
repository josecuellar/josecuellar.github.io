> Every observation, every conversation and every evolution strategy begins here.

## Understanding systems through situations

> During the last decades we have tried to understand human systems by observing only people changes.
> Human systems do not evolve only because people change.
> They evolve because situations enable de change.

We assess competencies.

We evaluate performance.

We measure productivity.

We discuss leadership.

We improve processes.

Yet two teams composed of exactly the same people can behave in completely different ways only a few weeks apart.

The explanation rarely lies in the people themselves.

More often, it lies in the **situation** the system is experiencing.

This simple idea is the foundation of the Situational Map and the conceptual core of SAMM.

The Situational Map is not a leadership framework.

It is not a maturity model.

It does not classify people.

It does not evaluate team performance.

Instead, it provides an **observational model** that helps us understand the context experienced by a human system before attempting to interpret it or intervene.

Because only by understanding the situation can we correctly interpret the energy we observe, have meaningful conversations and design appropriate evolution strategies.

Everything in SAMM originates from this map.

Snapshots capture the current state of the system.

Situational sensors detect relevant changes.

One-to-one conversations, coaching sessions, retrospectives and other feedback mechanisms validate the hypotheses produced through observation.

Finally, those observations become the foundation for deciding how the system should evolve.

The Situational Map does not attempt to tell us what we should do.

Its purpose is much simpler.

It helps us understand **what is actually happening** before deciding what to do next.

---

# Why was a Situational Map necessary?

Almost every modern management approach agrees on one fundamental idea.

**Context matters.**

Agile methodologies constantly remind us to adapt.

Leadership models encourage leaders to create the right environment for people to succeed.

Organizational psychology repeatedly shows that human behaviour depends not only on individuals, but also on the environment in which they operate.

Everyone talks about context.

Very few explain how to observe it.

How do we distinguish healthy growth from sustained overload?

How do we know whether a team needs more autonomy or stronger direction?

How can we differentiate a capability problem from a contextual problem?

Most methodologies provide tools for acting.

Very few provide a model for understanding the situation before acting.

SAMM was created to fill that gap.

Before intervening, it proposes observing.

Before deciding, it proposes understanding.

Because poor decisions rarely happen due to a lack of tools.

They usually happen because they begin with an incomplete diagnosis.

And every diagnosis starts by understanding the situation.

---

# The situation is what changes

When the behaviour of a system changes, we usually assume that people have changed.

Most of the time, they have not.

The situation has.

A reorganisation.

New team members.

A demanding customer.

A budget reduction.

A tight deadline.

The adoption of a new technology.

None of these events is a situation by itself.

They are simply events.

A situation emerges when those events modify the level of **challenge** experienced by the system.

This distinction is fundamental.

Two organisations may announce exactly the same organisational change.

One team may interpret it as an opportunity to grow.

Another may experience uncertainty and loss of direction.

The event is identical.

The situation is not.

For this reason, the Situational Map does not classify events.

It classifies the **situations** those events generate.

While an event describes something that happened, a situation describes the context from which people will respond to that event.

Situations are not sequential stages.

A system may move back and forth between them.

It may remain in the same situation for several observation cycles.

It may alternate repeatedly between two situations.

The purpose of the model is never to label a team.

Its purpose is to understand the context that explains its behaviour.

---

# The situation belongs to the system. Energy belongs to the actors.

This is one of the most important principles in SAMM.

Situations belong to the system.

Energy belongs to the people.

The same team may be experiencing **Pressure**, while each individual responds differently.

Some perceive the challenge as motivating.

Others begin to experience fatigue.

Some increase their contribution.

Others progressively disengage.

The situation is shared.

Energy is individual.

For this reason, the Situational Map never classifies people.

People contribute their [energy to the system](https://leadingdepth.com/the-energy-behind-every-human-system/).

Situations describe the context in which that energy is expressed.

Understanding both dimensions together provides a much more accurate picture of reality.

## Challenge, knowledge and Flow

Every situation represented in the Situational Map corresponds to a particular level of challenge.

However, challenge can never be interpreted in isolation.

Its effect depends entirely on the knowledge available within the system.

This idea is strongly supported by Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's theory of **Flow** explained in [The Energy Behind Every Human System](https://leadingdepth.com/the-energy-behind-every-human-system/)

According to Flow theory, people experience their highest levels of engagement when the challenge they face is well balanced with their capabilities.

When challenge is too low, boredom appears.

When challenge greatly exceeds available capabilities, anxiety appears.

Between those extremes lies the state where learning, motivation and performance reinforce one another.

SAMM adopts the same principle to understand human systems.

The Situational Map combines challenge and knowledge to describe the situations experienced by a system.

Different combinations of both dimensions produce different situations.

The map should not be interpreted as a timeline.

The wave does not represent time.

Its amplitude represents changes in the level of challenge experienced by the system.

Likewise, situations are not mandatory stages.

A system may revisit previous situations.

It may alternate between different situations.

Or it may remain within the same situation during several observation cycles.

The diagram is simply the most intuitive representation of the model.

Like any observational model, its value depends on the quality of observation.

The closer the observer is to the system, the better the hypotheses the model can generate.

And that is an important word.

**Hypotheses.**

The Situational Map does not produce absolute answers.

It produces hypotheses that are progressively validated through observation, conversations and situational sensors.

# The five situations

The Situational Map proposes five situations that describe different relationships between challenge and knowledge.

Together they provide a common observational language that helps explain how human systems evolve over time.

The boundaries between these situations are intentionally approximate.

Human systems are complex.

Transitions rarely occur at a precise moment.

The model therefore works with tendencies and probabilities rather than exact thresholds.

The following sections introduce each situation.

Future articles will explore each of them in much greater depth.

## Cruising Speed

Cruising Speed represents a healthy balance between challenge and knowledge.

The system operates at a sustainable pace, understands its objectives and still has enough capacity to continue growing without compromising its energy.

**This is a Learning & Growth Moment.** The system has enough stability and available energy to expand its knowledge, develop new capabilities and explore new challenges. For example, the team may learn a new technology, experiment with a different engineering practice or help its members grow into new responsibilities.

---

## Fluctuation

Fluctuation appears when challenge begins to increase.

The system starts adapting to new circumstances, learning accelerates and energy may temporarily fluctuate while equilibrium is maintained.

**This is a Practice & Analysis Moment.** The system needs opportunities to practise what it is learning and observe how it responds to the new challenge. For example, a team adopting a new delivery model may run small experiments, analyse their results and adjust its practices before applying the change more broadly.

---

## Pressure

Pressure emerges when challenge continuously exceeds the system's current capacity.

If maintained over time, pressure progressively affects the distribution of energy across the actors and increases the risk of exhaustion, reduced quality and loss of adaptability.

**This is a Fulfil Needs Moment.** The immediate priority is to identify and address the needs that the system can no longer satisfy under the current level of challenge. For example, the team may need more time, clearer decisions, additional knowledge, fewer simultaneous commitments or direct support to remove a critical impediment.

---

## Indirection

Sometimes the greatest challenge is not excessive workload but the absence of direction.

The system possesses enough capability to move forward, yet lacks sufficient clarity about where to focus its effort.

As a consequence, energy becomes dispersed and the collective potential of the system is only partially realised.

**This is a Review Purpose Moment.** The system needs to reconnect its activity with a meaningful and shared purpose. For example, the team may review why a product exists, clarify the outcome it is trying to achieve or reconsider priorities that no longer contribute to its goals.

---

## Stabilization

Following sustained periods of challenge, systems require time to consolidate learning and recover equilibrium.

Stabilization represents that recovery process, allowing energy to rebalance before new challenges are introduced.

The purpose of stabilization is not to stop evolving.

It is to ensure that future evolution remains sustainable.

**This is a Change Moment.** The system has an opportunity to incorporate what it has learned and make the changes required to establish a new sustainable balance. For example, it may redesign responsibilities, simplify a workflow, modify team agreements or adopt a different operating rhythm before facing the next challenge.


# Observing before intervening

One of the fundamental principles of SAMM is that **observation always precedes intervention**.

This may seem obvious, yet in practice most organisations tend to do exactly the opposite.

A team appears overwhelmed, so additional processes are introduced.

Communication deteriorates, so more meetings are scheduled.

Delivery slows down, so pressure increases.

Motivation declines, so new incentives are designed.

Very often, the intervention itself is not wrong.

The problem is that it is based on an incomplete understanding of the situation.

The Situational Map proposes a different approach.

Before deciding what should change, it asks a simpler question:

**What situation is this system actually experiencing?**

Only after answering that question does it become meaningful to discuss actions.

This is one of the reasons why the Situational Map should not be understood as a decision model.

It is an observational model.

Its role is not to prescribe solutions.

Its role is to generate a shared understanding of the system's current context.

---

## Situations require time to be observed

Human systems are dynamic.

Their behaviour fluctuates from one day to another.

Unexpected events occur continuously.

People have good days and bad days.

If observation is limited to isolated moments, almost any diagnosis becomes unreliable.

For this reason, SAMM recommends observing situations over **cycles of approximately two to four weeks**.

This recommendation is not arbitrary.

It aligns naturally with the cadence already adopted by many modern software development teams through Scrum iterations, Kanban review cycles or XP feedback practices.

A two-to-four-week observation window usually provides enough information to distinguish temporary fluctuations from genuine situational changes.

More importantly, it creates enough time for observation, conversation and intervention to become part of the same continuous learning cycle.

The recommendation should not be interpreted as a rigid rule.

Some systems evolve much faster.

Others require longer observation periods.

The important principle is not the exact duration.

The important principle is that situations should be observed long enough to reveal meaningful patterns rather than isolated events.

---

# Observation generates hypotheses

The Situational Map never claims absolute certainty.

Human systems are simply too complex for deterministic models.

Instead, SAMM works by generating progressively stronger hypotheses.

A system may appear to be entering **Pressure**.

Another may seem to be moving towards **Indirection**.

Those observations are not conclusions.

They are hypotheses that guide further observation.

This distinction is essential.

Many organisational models attempt to classify teams.

SAMM intentionally avoids that approach.

The objective is never to assign labels.

The objective is to improve understanding.

As new observations emerge, hypotheses evolve.

Sometimes they become stronger.

Sometimes they disappear completely.

The Situational Map therefore behaves less like a diagnostic report and more like a scientific observation process.

Every observation increases confidence.

Every conversation either reinforces or challenges previous assumptions.

Over time, the understanding of the system becomes progressively more accurate.

---

## Situational sensors

Observation requires evidence.

Without evidence, every interpretation becomes little more than intuition.

SAMM introduces **situational sensors** as observable signals that help understand how a system is evolving.

Unlike traditional KPIs, situational sensors are not intended to measure organisational success.

Their purpose is to detect changes in the behaviour of the system.

A sensor may reveal increasing uncertainty.

Another may indicate sustained overload.

Others may suggest improving collaboration, recovering direction or stabilising energy.

No individual sensor is capable of describing an entire situation.

Meaning emerges from the combination of multiple observations collected over time.

For this reason, sensors should never be interpreted independently.

Their value comes from the patterns they reveal together.

As those patterns become clearer, confidence in the situational hypothesis increases.

---

# Snapshots

Observation is continuous.

Decision-making, however, requires moments of synthesis.

SAMM refers to these observation points as **snapshots**.

A snapshot captures the current understanding of the system at a particular moment.

It is not intended to freeze reality.

Human systems continue evolving immediately after the snapshot is taken.

Instead, snapshots provide a shared reference from which meaningful conversations can begin.

They answer questions such as:

- What situation do we currently believe the system is experiencing?
- How has that situation evolved since the previous observation?
- Which sensors changed significantly?
- What hypotheses became stronger?
- Which assumptions should now be reconsidered?

Because snapshots represent understanding rather than certainty, they are expected to evolve.

A future snapshot may completely replace today's interpretation.

That is not a failure of the model.

It is evidence that the observation process is working.

---

## Conversations validate observation

Observation alone is never sufficient.

Human systems cannot be fully understood through external observation.

People experience situations differently.

Their perspectives provide essential context that no sensor can fully capture.

For this reason, conversations occupy a central role within SAMM.

One-to-one meetings.

Coaching sessions.

Retrospectives.

Leadership conversations.

Team discussions.

Each provides additional evidence that helps validate—or challenge—the current situational hypothesis.

Importantly, these conversations are not separate activities.

They are direct extensions of the observation process.

Observation generates questions.

Conversations generate understanding.

Together they progressively improve the quality of the situational model.

---

# From understanding to intervention

Observation is never the final objective.

Its purpose is to support better decisions.

Once sufficient confidence exists about the current situation, SAMM can begin designing **situational intervention strategies**.

This is where the Situational Map differs significantly from many organisational frameworks.

Most models recommend a fixed set of practices.

SAMM proposes something different.

Different situations require different interventions.

A team experiencing **Pressure** rarely benefits from the same actions as a team experiencing **Indirection**.

Likewise, a system in **Stabilization** should not be managed in the same way as one operating at **Cruising Speed**.

The intervention therefore emerges from the observed situation rather than from predefined organisational recipes.

This principle also explains why situational sensors are so important.

They do not merely support observation.

They guide change.

As sensors evolve, intervention strategies evolve with them.

Observation, conversation, validation and intervention become part of the same continuous feedback cycle.

Rather than prescribing universal solutions, SAMM continuously adapts its understanding of the system and adjusts its recommendations accordingly.

This continuous cycle of observation and adaptation forms the operational core of the Situational Map.

The following section explores one final concept that makes this possible: **shared energy and situational memory**.


## Shared energy

Until now we have discussed situations as properties of the system.

However, situations alone cannot explain everything that happens inside a human system.

To understand why, we need to return to one of the core ideas introduced earlier in this series:

**Energy belongs to actors.**

Each actor contributes a unique energy to every system they participate in.

That energy does not belong exclusively to work.

Nor does it belong exclusively to personal life.

It belongs to the individual.

This distinction has profound implications.

A professional situation characterised by sustained **Pressure** may reduce the energy that same individual brings into family life.

Likewise, prolonged **Indirection** in someone's personal environment may reduce the energy they contribute to their professional system.

The situations are different.

The energy is shared.

This means that professional and personal systems cannot be understood as completely independent realities.

They continuously influence one another through the actors they share.

SAMM therefore observes situations at the system level while recognising that the energy carried by individuals naturally flows across every system in which they participate.

Understanding this relationship allows leaders, coaches and teams to interpret behavioural changes with much greater accuracy.

Sometimes the origin of an observable change lies outside the system currently being observed.

---

## Situational memory

Observation does not begin from zero.

Every system carries a memory of the situations it has previously experienced.

Two teams may currently appear to be in exactly the same situation.

Their future evolution, however, may be completely different.

Why?

Because they arrived there through different paths.

One team may have recently recovered from several months of sustained **Pressure**.

Another may have gradually evolved from **Cruising Speed** through a brief period of **Fluctuation**.

Although both observations appear similar today, their situational memories are completely different.

SAMM refers to this accumulated trajectory as **situational memory**.

Situational memory provides essential context for interpreting the present.

The current situation explains where the system is.

Situational memory explains how it arrived there.

Without this historical perspective, interventions risk treating identical observations as identical problems.

In reality, they rarely are.

# Why trajectory matters

Situational memory also explains why identical interventions often produce different outcomes.

Imagine two teams currently experiencing **Pressure**.

One has maintained healthy rhythms for months before entering this situation.

The other has already spent several months under sustained overload.

Although both appear similar today, introducing additional challenge will probably affect them very differently.

The first team may still perceive challenge as stimulating.

The second may experience the same intervention as overwhelming.

Current observation alone cannot explain that difference.

Trajectory can.

This is why SAMM never evaluates the present in isolation.

Every observation includes the path that produced it.

---

# Learned Helplessness

This principle is consistent with Martin Seligman's work on **Learned Helplessness**.

When individuals repeatedly experience situations where their actions appear unable to change outcomes, they gradually reduce initiative, experimentation and engagement.

The external circumstances may eventually improve.

Behaviour often takes much longer to recover.

Human systems behave similarly.

A team that remains under prolonged **Pressure** or extended **Indirection** does not immediately recover simply because the situation changes.

Its previous experience continues influencing how new situations are interpreted.

Situational memory therefore becomes an essential part of understanding recovery.

Changing the context is often necessary.

It is rarely sufficient by itself.

---

## Yerkes-Dodson and adaptive challenge

A similar conclusion can be drawn from the Yerkes-Dodson law explained in [The Energy Behind Every Human System](https://leadingdepth.com/the-energy-behind-every-human-system/)

Performance does not increase indefinitely as challenge increases.

Beyond a certain point, additional challenge begins reducing performance instead of improving it.

SAMM extends this idea by introducing situational memory.

The amount of challenge introduced into a system should not depend only on its current situation.

It should also depend on its recent trajectory.

Two systems displaying similar energy distributions today may require completely different intervention strategies.

One may benefit from additional challenge.

The other may first require recovery.

Understanding where a system has been becomes just as important as understanding where it is.

# Beyond organisations

Although the examples throughout this article primarily describe professional environments, the Situational Map was never designed exclusively for organisations.

Any human system experiences situations.

Families.

Sports teams.

Volunteer communities.

Educational environments.

Friendship groups.

Startups.

Large enterprises.

The situations may differ.

The observation principles remain remarkably consistent.

Because the model focuses on relationships between challenge, knowledge and energy rather than organisational structures, its underlying concepts remain applicable across many different kinds of human systems.

---

# The heart of SAMM

The Situational Map is much more than a diagram.

It provides the observational language upon which every other component of SAMM is built.

Snapshots.

Situational sensors.

One-to-one conversations.

Coaching.

Retrospectives.

Leadership observations.

Intervention strategies.

Everything begins here.

Not because the map predicts behaviour.

But because it helps us observe behaviour before attempting to explain it.

Like every observational model, it produces hypotheses rather than certainty.

Those hypotheses become progressively stronger through continuous observation, conversations and situational memory.

Ultimately, the purpose of the Situational Map is not to describe human systems.

Its purpose is to help us understand them.

Because better understanding almost always precedes better decisions.

And better decisions are what allow human systems to evolve sustainably.
